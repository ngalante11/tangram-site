Observation-Ready

Danielson Domain 3 Lesson Examples Your Evaluator Will Actually Recognize

7 min read · Tangram Blog

Domain 3 — Instruction — is where observations are won or lost. Domains 1 and 2 happen before the bell and around the edges; Domain 3 is the lesson itself, live, while someone with a rubric sits in the back of your room. It's also the domain most teachers get dinged on, not because their teaching is weak, but because the evidence of good teaching isn't visible in the moment.

Here's a component-by-component breakdown of Domain 3 with concrete, classroom-ready examples — the kind of moves an evaluator can actually see and check off.

A quick map of Domain 3

Charlotte Danielson's Framework for Teaching breaks Domain 3 into five components:

  • 3a — Communicating with students
  • 3b — Using questioning and discussion techniques
  • 3c — Engaging students in learning
  • 3d — Using assessment in instruction
  • 3e — Demonstrating flexibility and responsiveness

Let's make each one observable.

3a — Communicating with students

What the evaluator wants to see: the purpose of the lesson is clear, directions are crisp, and your explanations meet students where they are.

Do this:

  • Post and say the objective in student-friendly language: "By the end of today, you'll be able to cite two pieces of evidence that support the author's central idea."
  • Give directions in no more than three steps, and check them: "Tell your partner step one." (Now the evaluator sees comprehension, not just compliance.)
  • Use precise academic vocabulary and define it in the moment.

3b — Questioning and discussion

What the evaluator wants to see: high-quality questions, real student-to-student discussion, and wait time — not you calling on the same three hands.

Do this:

  • Plan 2–3 genuinely open questions in advance (write them into the lesson). "What evidence would change your mind?" beats "Does everyone understand?"
  • Use a discussion protocol: think-pair-share, or "add on to what your classmate said."
  • Build in wait time and cold-call with equity sticks or a randomizer so participation isn't volunteer-only.

3c — Engaging students in learning

3c is the heart of the domain and the one people ask about most. "Engagement" here does not mean students are entertained — it means they're doing the intellectual work.

What the evaluator wants to see: students cognitively busy, appropriately challenged, with the pacing and grouping serving the learning.

Do this:

  • Design the task so students do more work than you do. If you're talking more than they're thinking, that's a 3c problem.
  • Differentiate the task so it's challenging for every group — your honors kids aren't coasting and your ELLs aren't locked out. (Same objective, different access.)
  • Make the "product" of the lesson visible: an annotation, a claim with evidence, a solved problem they can defend.

3d — Using assessment in instruction

What the evaluator wants to see: you're checking for understanding during the lesson and adjusting — not just collecting an exit ticket to grade later.

Do this:

  • Plan the checks in advance: a mid-lesson mini-whiteboard, a quick poll, a targeted question at a known misconception.
  • Give feedback that moves learning forward in the moment: "You've got the claim — now where's your evidence?"
  • Show students the success criteria so they can self-assess.

3e — Demonstrating flexibility and responsiveness

What the evaluator wants to see: when the lesson hits a snag, you adjust — you have a plan B and you read the room.

Do this:

  • Pre-plan an "if they're not getting it" branch and an "if they've got it, go deeper" branch. (This is differentiation showing up live.)
  • Seize a teachable moment and name why you're pivoting.
  • Never keep sailing a lesson that's clearly underwater. Adjusting is the evidence.

The hard part isn't knowing this — it's building it into every lesson

Read that list and it's obvious: plan open questions, plan your checks, plan your branches, differentiate the task. The problem is doing all of it, for every lesson, for every class — while also, you know, teaching.

That's the gap between knowing the framework and being observation-ready on a random Tuesday.
