How to Differentiate One Lesson for a Mixed-Ability Class (Without Rewriting It 5 Times)

6 min read · Tangram Blog

You wrote a good lesson. It's tight, it hits the standard, and in Period 1 it sings.

Then Period 3 walks in — eight of your students are still building English, and the independent reading passage you chose might as well be in another language. Period 5 is honors, and they finished the "challenge" question in four minutes and are now, quietly, running your classroom. By the end of the day you've taught the same lesson five times and none of them were quite right.

So Sunday night, you do it again. You take your one good lesson and you rebuild it — a simpler version here, an extension there, a word bank for your ELLs, a scaffolded graphic organizer for the students with IEPs. Five versions of one lesson. Every week.

There's a better way to do this, and it starts with a mindset shift: you should only have to build the lesson once. Differentiation isn't a fifth, sixth, and seventh lesson — it's a set of adjustments to a single strong one. Here's how to think about it, and how to do it without losing your weekends.

First, separate the lesson from its versions

The reason planning eats your Sunday is that most teachers (and most planning tools) treat each class as a separate build. It isn't. In a well-differentiated lesson, the core stays fixed:

  • the standard and learning objective
  • the essential question
  • the concept you're teaching
  • the way you'll check for understanding

What changes between classes is the access — how each group gets to that same objective:

  • the reading level of the text
  • the amount of scaffolding on the task
  • the language support (word banks, sentence frames, native-language glossaries)
  • the grouping and pacing
  • the extension for students who are ready to go deeper

Once you see a lesson as "one fixed core + adjustable access," differentiation stops being five lessons and becomes one lesson with dials.

The 3-column planning method

Here's a method you can use today with nothing but a doc and your class rosters. Draw three columns for any part of the lesson that varies:

Below grade levelOn grade levelAbove grade level
Simplified text + word bankOriginal textOriginal text + primary source
Sentence frames for the responseOpen responseResponse + counter-argument
Guided graphic organizerPartial organizerBlank / student-designed organizer

Now overlay your actual students:

  • ELLs pull from the left column for text and language support, but don't dumb down the thinking — they get the same objective with more access.
  • Students with IEPs — match the accommodation to the goal (extended time, chunked task, organizer), not a watered-down objective.
  • Honors / ready-for-more — the right column keeps them in the same lesson instead of "finish early and wait."

The objective never moves. Only the path to it does.

Where this method breaks down (and why)

The 3-column method is genuinely good. It's also genuinely exhausting to do by hand for every lesson, every week, across five classes. That's the honest catch — and it's why so many teachers know how to differentiate but don't have time to actually do it.

Doing it manually means:

  • Rewriting the passage at two or three reading levels
  • Building the sentence frames and word banks from scratch
  • Re-cutting the graphic organizer for each tier
  • Keeping track of which student needs which version

Multiply that by every lesson and it's a second job.
