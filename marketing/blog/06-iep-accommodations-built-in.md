# IEP Accommodations Built Into Your Lesson Plan (Not Bolted On)

Most accommodations get added last. You write the lesson, and then — as an afterthought,
usually the night before — you go back and figure out how to make it work for your
students with IEPs. Extended time here, a graphic organizer there, a note to read the
directions aloud.

It works, sort of. But bolting accommodations onto a finished lesson is harder, slower,
and easier to get wrong than building them in from the start. Here's how to flip the
order — and why it makes your planning both more compliant and less painful.

## Why "bolt-on" accommodations fail you

When accommodations are an afterthought, three things go wrong:

1. **They get forgotten.** A finished lesson doesn't remind you that Marcus needs chunked
   text. If it's not built into the plan, it lives in your memory — and your memory is
   full.
2. **They don't fit.** Retrofitting extended time onto a lesson designed to end exactly
   at the bell means someone's cutting the closure. Accommodations bolted on collide with
   a plan that didn't leave room for them.
3. **They're not documented.** When the accommodation isn't visible in your plan, you
   have nothing to point to that shows you provided it — which matters legally and during
   observations.

Building accommodations in from the start solves all three at once.

## Match the accommodation to the barrier, not the student's label

The first move isn't "what does the IEP say" — it's "where in *this* lesson will this
student hit a wall?" Then you match the accommodation to that specific barrier. A few
common ones, mapped to where they belong in the lesson:

- **Presentation barriers** (can't access the input): read directions aloud, chunk the
  text, provide a copy of notes, add visuals. Build these into the *input* section.
- **Response barriers** (knows it, can't show it the standard way): allow oral responses,
  sentence starters, a scribe, typed instead of handwritten. Build these into the *task*.
- **Setting/timing barriers** (needs different conditions): extended time, a quiet space,
  frequent breaks, preferential seating. Build these into the *pacing and grouping*.

The IEP tells you *what* is required. The lesson is where you decide *how* — and that
decision belongs in the plan, not in a sticky note.

## Design the lesson so accommodations are the default, not the exception

Here's the mindset shift that makes this sustainable: a lot of "accommodations" are just
good design that helps everyone. When you build them in as the default, you're doing less
special-casing.

- Post directions in writing *and* say them aloud — now the read-aloud accommodation is
  automatic, and your whole class benefits.
- Give a partially-completed graphic organizer as the standard task, with a fully-guided
  version ready — the scaffold is a dial, not a separate worksheet.
- Build in a natural checkpoint partway through — extended-time students get a clean place
  to continue, and everyone gets a check for understanding.

You're still honoring the specific, legally-required accommodations for each student. You've
just designed a lesson that absorbs most of them without a separate build.

## The honest catch: doing this for every student, every lesson

Every part of this is doable. It's the *volume* that breaks teachers. Cross-reference
every IEP against every section of every lesson, for every class, every day — and keep it
documented — and you've added a second job to the one you already have. Which is exactly
why accommodations end up bolted on at 10pm: not because teachers don't know better, but
because there's no time to do it right by hand.
