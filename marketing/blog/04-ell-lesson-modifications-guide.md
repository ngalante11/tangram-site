# ELL Lesson Modifications: A Section-by-Section Guide

There's a myth that modifying a lesson for English language learners means making it
*easier*. It doesn't. The goal is never a lower bar — it's more **access** to the same
bar. Your ELLs can do the grade-level thinking; what they need is support getting to it
while they're still building the language.

The trouble is that "provide scaffolds and supports" is advice, not a plan. So here's the
plan: a section-by-section walkthrough of a typical lesson, with concrete modifications
you can drop into each part. Same objective throughout. Only the access changes.

## Before you modify anything: know your levels

"ELL" is not one thing. A newcomer at an entering proficiency level needs very different
support than a student who's nearly fluent in conversation but still developing academic
language. Whatever framework your state uses (WIDA levels, for example), sort your
supports by proficiency so a newcomer isn't drowning and an advanced student isn't bored.

A quick mental model: **the lower the proficiency, the more you support the language
without touching the thinking.**

## The objective and success criteria

**Keep the objective identical.** Change how it's communicated.

- Post the objective in student-friendly language and pair it with a visual or an icon.
- Pre-teach the 3–5 words a student *must* have to access the lesson (not a giant
  vocabulary dump — the load-bearing words).
- Give the success criteria as a concrete example of what a strong response looks like,
  not just a description of it.

## The warm-up / activating prior knowledge

This is where ELLs are most often locked out — the warm-up assumes cultural or background
knowledge they may not share.

- Use an image, a short video clip, or a real object as the entry point instead of a
  text-only prompt.
- Let students activate knowledge in their home language first, then bridge to English.
- Offer a sentence starter so the response is about the *thinking*, not the struggle to
  begin: "This reminds me of ______ because ______."

## The main text or input

The reading is usually the biggest barrier — and the biggest opportunity.

- Provide the text at an adjusted reading level *without* cutting the core ideas.
- Build a word bank pulled directly from the passage, with student-friendly definitions
  and, where helpful, a first-language glossary.
- Chunk the text and add a one-line summary or a visual beside each chunk.
- Model annotation once, out loud, so students see the thinking process, not just the
  finished markup.

## The task / practice

The task is where the real learning happens, so protect the cognitive demand and support
the output.

- Provide sentence frames and paragraph frames that carry the academic structure:
  "The author argues ______. One piece of evidence is ______, which shows ______."
- Let students show understanding in more than one mode — a labeled diagram, a
  think-aloud, a short spoken response — before requiring a polished written one.
- Use strategic grouping: pair an ELL with a supportive, patient peer, or group by home
  language for the thinking phase and bridge to English for the share-out.

## The check for understanding

Don't let a language barrier disguise itself as a content gap.

- Separate what you're assessing (the concept) from how you're assessing it (in English).
  A quick visual, a thumbs signal, or a first-language check can tell you what a
  paragraph can't.
- Give extra wait time. Processing in a second language takes longer — that's not
  hesitation, it's cognition.

## The extension

Yes — ELLs get extensions too. "More access" is not "less challenge."

- Offer a deeper question with the same scaffolds attached.
- Invite bilingual students to compare how an idea is expressed across languages — a
  genuine analytical task that honors their assets.

## The honest problem: doing this for every lesson

Read that list and every item is reasonable. Now do all of it — for every lesson, for
every ELL, at three different proficiency levels, on top of the differentiation your IEP
students and your honors kids also need. By hand, that's not a modification. It's a
second full lesson plan, every day.

That's the gap between knowing what good ELL support looks like and actually delivering
it when you have five periods and a stack of grading.
